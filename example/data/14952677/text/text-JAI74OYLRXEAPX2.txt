Response to Arguments
Applicant's arguments with respect to claims 1-9 and 11-19 have been considered have been fully considered but they are not persuasive because of the following:
35 USC 101 Issue:
Regarding claim 1, the applicant argues on pages 7-11 that there is no 101 issue in the claim. It is because, the claim as a whole has no abstract idea and reference to a non-precedential ruling. Examiner respectfully disagrees with this argument. It is because, the claimed a plurality of wireless strand speakers with additional lighting features device are generic components and they perform their basic functions of connecting/coupling wireless speakers with the lights through a hardwired connection. Thus claim allegedly demonstrates that the concept is well known and abstract. According to the Federal Circuit, the implementation of the abstract idea is routine and conventional, and does not “improve the functioning of the speaker or strand speakers with additional lighting features itself” (see Intellectual Ventures v. Symantec ‘142 patent).
Thus, the rejection of the claims will remain.
Regarding 35 USC 103 rejection:
Regarding claim 1, the applicant argues on pages 12-13 that neither Lin nor Maker teach or suggest a string. Examiner respectfully disagrees with this argument. It is because, the applicant did not define the claimed "string" in the claim. Instead, the applicant claims placing lights in or on a string. Lin teaches connecting speakers through hard wire (see fig.1, 2; paragraphs 0014-0015) whereas Maker teaches connecting speakers and lights through bus or any kind of connections such as Ethernet connection (see fig.12, item 1220; col.9, line 66-col.10, line 11).
Thus, the rejection of the claim will remain.
Regarding official notices of claims 4, 7-9, 14, 16-19, the applicant further argues on pages 13-15 in order to traverse the official notices. It is because neither of the official notices are based upon common knowledge. Furthermore, Applicant is challenging the factual assertion made by the Examiner as not properly based upon common knowledge, Applicant is requesting that Examiner to support the finding with adequate documentary evidence in the next Office action if the rejection is to be maintained as provided by MPEP §2144.03(B), MPEP §2144.03(C) and also see Zurko. Examiner respectfully disagrees with this argument. It is because, the official notices are used based on common knowledge. For example, in claim 4, the applicant claims that the speakers are placed every 4 to 5 feet apart along the light string. The examiner takes an official notice that the speakers are being placed every 4 to 5 feet apart along the light string is a matter of design choice. It is because, a change in size is generally recognized as being within the level of ordinary skill in the art. In re Rose, 105 USPQ 237 (CCPA 1955). Further, in Gardner v. TEC Systems, Inc., 725 F.2d 1338, 220 USPQ 777 (Fed. Cir. 1984), cert. denied, 469 U.S. 830, 225 USPQ 232 (1984), the Federal Circuit held that, where the only difference between the prior art and the claims was a recitation of relative dimensions of the claimed device and a device having the claimed relative dimensions would not perform differently than the prior art device, the claimed device was not patentably distinct from the prior art device.
Thus, the rejection of the claims will remain.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 101
35 U.S.C. 101 reads as follows:
Whoever invents or discovers any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement thereof, may obtain a patent therefor, subject to the conditions and requirements of this title.
Claims 1-9 and 11-19 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 101 because the claimed invention is directed to a judicial exception (i.e., a law of nature, a natural phenomenon, or an abstract idea) without significantly more. Claim 1 is directed to a plurality of wireless strand speakers with additional lighting features device comprising: a plurality of speakers strung together in combination forming a string of speakers. The claim 1 does not include additional elements that are sufficient to amount to significantly more than the judicial exception because the claim requires additional limitations of a plurality of wireless strand speakers with additional lighting features device comprising: a plurality of speakers strung together in combination forming a string of speakers. The generic components are claimed to perform their basic functions of connecting/coupling wireless speakers with the lights through a hardwired connection. The recitation of limitations amounts to mere instructions to implement the abstract idea on the electronic device. As such, there is no inventive concept sufficient to transform the claimed subject matter into a patent-eligible application. The claimed does not amount to significantly more than the abstract idea itself. Accordingly, the claim is not patent eligible.
Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103(a) which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
(a) A patent may not be obtained though the invention is not identically disclosed or described as set forth in section 102 of this title, if the differences between the subject matter sought to be patented and the prior art are such that the subject matter as a whole would have been obvious at the time the invention was made to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which said subject matter pertains. Patentability shall not be negatived by the manner in which the invention was made.
The factual inquiries set forth in Graham v. John Deere Co., 383 U.S. 1, 148 USPQ 459 (1966), that are applied for establishing a background for determining obviousness under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) are summarized as follows:
1. Determining the scope and contents of the prior art.
2. Ascertaining the differences between the prior art and the claims at issue.
3. Resolving the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art.
4. Considering objective evidence present in the application indicating obviousness or nonobviousness.

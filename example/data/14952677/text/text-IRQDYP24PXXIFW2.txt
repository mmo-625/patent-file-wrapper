Rejections under 35 USC 102 Claims 1-3, 5 and 6 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 102(a)(2) as being anticipated by Lin (U.S. Pub. No. 2014/0219481).
With respect to claim 1, the Examiner alleges Figures 1-2 of Lin teach a strand speaker device comprising all the claim limitations.
The Examiner alleges (fig. 1, 2; paragraphs 0014-0015) teach a plurality of speakers strung together in combination and all the limitations of claim 1. Lin, in para. 14 actually states and requires that a sensing switch 106 be disposed between each speaker in his wired or unwired string of speakers. Thus, Lin does not teach an identical invention nor every element of the claimed invention as arranged in the claim. Here Lin would not have the same invention and would not be functional in the same manner if the sensing switches 106 were omitted.
Lin does not teach the two limitations where the first speaker on the string is wireless, using wireless technology to obtain input (fig. 1, 2; paragraphs 0014-0015); and the first speaker on the string is wired to a plurality of additional speakers hardwired to each other (fig. 1, 2;
paragraphs 0014-0015). Para. 14 of Lin states:
"Generally, the first speaker 102 and the second speaker 104 are connected together to form a single sound channel or dual sound channels speaker. As shown in FIG. 1b, when the first speaker 102 is separated from the second speaker 104, the sensing switch 106, disposed between the first speaker 102 and the second speaker 104, outputs a signal to the audio decoder 108.
Therefore, the audio decoder 108 switches the multiple sound channels speaker 100 from a single sound channel or dual sound channels speaker to a 2.1 sound channels speaker, and switches the two second speakers 104 to left and right sound channels speakers respectively, and further switches the first speaker 102 to a low-sounding speaker."
Here, Lin is not teaching a string of speakers where the first speaker on the string is wired to a plurality of additional speakers hardwired to each other and the first speaker on the string is wireless, using wireless technology to obtain input. Lin is teaching a speaker system that requires and uses a sensing switch 106 that is disposed between the first speaker 102 and the second speaker 104, outputs a signal to the audio decoder 108. Where the audio decoder 108 then determines and switches the sound channels of the various speakers to determine and switch or set the plurality of speakers to left or right sound channels and even "low-sounding" speakers.
The present invention, in comparison, has no such switch and decoder, and the invention of Lin would be non-functional for its intended purpose if altered in a manner to remove or not use the audio decoder and sensing switches. Here, the present invention merely has one or more speakers, which play the music as received by the first speaker, which have wired or wireless communication with a device providing the audio.
With respect to claims 2 and 3, the limitation specifically states an auxiliary (AUX) connection used connect a first set of string of speakers to a second set of string speakers. Here, Lin does not teach sets of string speakers but a speaker system which may or may not have the speakers wired together to create a single set of speakers.
Lin makes no teaching or suggestion that could be viewed as anticipating the creation of speaker "strings" as taught and claimed by the present invention or the combining or linking of such speakers strings (claim 3) as taught and claimed by the present invention as their use is in conflict with that of Lin.
The Examiner cites Figures 1 and 2 as teaching this limitation, but figures 1 and 2 make no mention of and aux cord and or an aux port. In fact, and entire reading and word search of Line fails to find any mention or use of "aux", "port" or "auxiliary port". Thus, Lin makes no teaching or suggestion of an AUX plug, port, or connection between one or more strands of speakers, which is logical since Lin does not teach or suggestion the creating of speaker strands as disclosed and claimed by the present invention and would therefore not be able to teach or suggest the connection of one or more strands, since strands are not used or taught by Lin in any manner, as Lin is direct to creating a speaker system that can be switched between two or more various sound channels to match speaker performance with that of the audio broadcast being received.
With respect to claims 5 and 6, the limitations are "wherein on one end of the string of speakers will have a male auxiliary (AUX) in connection to connect to additional speaker sets" and "wherein on the opposing end of the speaker string, a female auxiliary (AUX) in connection for non-wired audio devices to connect to the speakers is provided".
In claims 5 and 6 of the present invention the limitations are narrow and well defined specifically claiming how one or more "strings" of speakers is to be connected in a wired embodiment and specifying the beginning and ending wiring of each string so that they can be connected and interchangeable.
Lin makes no teaching or suggestion that could be viewed as anticipating the creation of speaker "strings" as taught and claimed by the present invention or the combining or linking of such speakers strings (claim 3) as taught and claimed by the present invention as their use is in conflict with that of Lin. MPEP § 2131 states that "'[a] claim is anticipated only if each and every element as set forth in the claim is found, either expressly or inherently [See related post] described, in a single prior art reference.' Verdegaal Bros. v. Union Oil Co. of California, 814 F.2d 628, 631, 2 USPQ2d 1051, 1053 (Fed. Cir. 1987)" (emphasis added). "'The identical invention must be shown in as complete detail as is contained in the ... claim.' Richardson v. Suzuki Motor Co., 868 F.2d 1226, 1236, 9 USPQ2d 1913, 1920 (Fed. Cir. 1989)" (emphasis added).
Moreover, "[e]very element of the claimed invention must be literally present, arranged as in the claim." Id. (emphasis added). In the present case, the Office Action has not established that each element of the claims is disclosed in Lin.
Additionally, it appears that the United States Supreme Court values "reasons" in a rejection, and would likely require that such reasoning be applied to rejections under 35 U.S.C. § 102 if presented with the issue, rather than just rejections under 35 U.S.C. § 103. For instance, MPEP § 2143, citing KSR, states that "[t]he key to supporting any rejection under 35 U.S.C. 103 is the clear articulation of the reason(s) why the claimed invention would have been obvious. The Supreme Court in KSR noted that the analysis supporting a rejection under 35 U.S.C. 103 should be made explicit" (emphasis added, see also KSR Intl Co. v. Teleflex Inc., 82 USPQ2d 1385, 1395-97 (2007)). While KSR and the cited section of the MPEP are directed to obviousness

## rejections under 35 U.S.C. § 103, it is apparent that the Supreme Court believes that a clear
articulation of reasons is "the key" to supportable rejections by the USPTO.
For the reasons discussed above, and more specifically, because the claim limitations of the present invention are not shown in as complete detail as is contained in the claims and every element of the claimed invention is not literally presented and arranged as in the claims by Lin, withdrawal of these rejections are respectfully requested.

Rejections under 35 USC 103 A prima facie case of obviousness is established when an examiner provides:
1. one or more references 2. that were available to the inventor and 3. that teach 4. a suggestion to combine or modify the references, 5. the combination or modification of which would appear to be sufficient to have made the claimed invention obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art.
Accordingly, an applicant who is able to prove that the Examiner has failed to establish any one of these elements will prevent the prima facie case of obviousness from being established.
Under Graham v. John Deere Co. the standard of obvious used by the Examiner to support the obviousness rejection is incorrect. The Examiner must view the claim as a whole and not merely the differences between the present invention and the prior art. Here, Examiner has done nothing more with respect to the other than to list the claim elements/limitations, and merely citing a teaching of the individual element in the prior art. Thus, it is not correct for an Examiner to focus on the differences between the prior art and the claimed invention and then to state that the differences themselves or individually are obvious, there must be some expectation that the combination of the two would be successful and there must be motivation to combine.
Here, there is no expectation that merely combining the two systems would be easily and readily apparent to one of ordinary skill in the art. If such a combination is obvious and easily enabled, Examiner should quickly and easily be able to provide a citation for just such a combination.
Claims 4 and 7-9 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over Lin (U.S. Pub. No. 2014/0219481).
With respect to claims 4 and 8, Section 2144.03 of the Manual of Patent Examining Procedure (MPEP) provides guidelines for relying on Official Notice and personal knowledge.
Official Notice is permissible only in limited circumstances. Official Notice is permissible only for certain types of facts; those that are capable of instant and unquestionable demonstration as being well-known. It is never appropriate to rely solely on Official Notice as the principal evidence upon which a rejection was based. Instead, Official Notice is only appropriate for facts that are of notorious character and that serve to fill in the gaps that might exist in the evidentiary showing to support a particular ground of rejection. (MPEP § 2144.03(A)).
To traverse Official Notice, an Applicant must specifically point out the supposed error(s) in the Examiner's action, which would include stating why the noticed fact is not considered to be common knowledge or well-known in the art. Here, the Examiner has cited related prior art of Lin and Maker, neither of which teach placing lights in or on a string. Lin teaches a method for speaker switching and Maker a method for creating a presentation setting using multiple lights and speakers, not on a string but connected by a serial bus and set about a room.
With respect to claim 4, none of the cited prior art teaches a string, which is why the Examiner can not readily find any prior art teaching with respect to the limitations "the speakers should have between 1%2 Watt to 5 Watts each; and the speakers are placed every 4 to 5 feet apart along the light string", which are directed to the spacing of the lights and speakers on a string and their desired size range for optimal performance. The prior art does not use, teach, or suggest the novel and non-obvious combination taught by the present invention which is why the Examiner can not readily find prior art teaching these limitations and is taking unsupported Official Notice in an effort to improperly reject the claims.
With respect to claim 8, none of the cited prior art suggests a battery back as both Lin and Maker required much more sophisticated electronics to run their devices and could not be enabled easily by a battery pack. It would not have been obvious to a POSTIA as alleged by the Examiner in their reasoning for a POSITA to use a battery pack for the devices taught by Lin or Maker since both require additional electronics and power to run, where the use of a battery pack to power them would make them non-functional for their intended purpose.
The Examiner must provide specific factual findings predicated on sound technical and scientific reasoning to support taking Official Notice. (MPEP §2144.03(B)). The Examiner should present Applicant with the explicit basis on which Official Notice is based so that the Applicant is able to challenge the assertion in the next reply after the Office action. (MPEP §2144.03(B)).
Again, here Examiner has made no assertion of the explicit basis on which Official Notice has been taken. An Examiner may rely on facts within his or her own knowledge to support a rejection. When relying on personal knowledge, however, the Examiner must provide an affidavit or declaration setting forth specific factual statements and an explanation to support the finding. (MPEP §2144.03(C)). Such an affidavit is subject to contradiction or explanation by the affidavits of the Applicant and/or other persons. (See 37 CFR §1.104(d)(2)).
When an Applicant challenges a factual assertion as not properly officially noticed or not properly based upon common knowledge, the Office must support the finding with adequate documentary evidence in the next Office action if the rejection is to be maintained. (MPEP §2144.03(C)).
At this time Applicant is challenging the factual assertion made by the Examiner as not properly officially noticed and not properly based upon common knowledge, Applicant is requesting that Examiner support the finding with adequate documentary evidence in the next Office action if the rejection is to be maintained as provided by MPEP §2144.03(B), MPEP §2144.03(C) and also see Zurko ("holding that general conclusions concerning what is 'basic knowledge' or 'common sense' to one of ordinary skill in the art without specific factual findings and some concrete evidence in the record to support these findings will not support an obviousness rejection"). Examiner is requested to provide an affidavit/declaration under 37 CFR 1.104(d)(2) or withdraw the rejection.
Additionally, Official Notice is not sufficient to supply motivation for modifications.
The factual inquire to modify must be based on objective evidence of records. Ex parte Humphreys holds that when an Examiner does not provide specific reasons to support the obviousness rejection, they have failed to establish the prima facie case of obviousness.
Here the Examiner fails to state the specific element or motivation that is being taken for each of the respective claim limitations and merely makes up a reason stating "it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made to modify Lin to incorporate the speakers having between 4 Watt to 5 Watts each and the speakers are placed every 4 to 5 feet apart along the light string in Lin's invention in order to make particular speaker device of a particular power and particular distance", which could just as easily be validated by making a statement that any characteristic is inherent.
With respect to claims 4 and 8, MPEP § 2143 states that "[t]he key to supporting any

## rejection under 35 U.S.C. 103 is the clear articulation of the reason(s) why the claimed
invention would have been obvious. The Supreme Court in KSR noted that the analysis supporting a rejection under 35 U.S.C. 103 should be made explicit" (emphasis added).
According to MPEP § 2143, rationales that may support a conclusion of obviousness include: (A) Combining prior art elements according to known methods to yield predictable results;
(B) Simple substitution of one known element for another to obtain predictable results;
(C) Use of known technique to improve similar devices (methods, or products) in the same way;
(D) Applying a known technique to a known device (method, or product) ready for improvement to yield predictable results;
(E) "Obvious to try" - choosing from a finite number of identified, predictable solutions, with a reasonable expectation of success;
(F) Known work in one field of endeavor may prompt variations of it for use in either the same field or a different one based on design incentives or other market forces if the variations are predictable to one of ordinary skill in the art;
(G) Some teaching, suggestion, or motivation in the prior art that would have led one of ordinary skill to modify the prior art reference or to combine prior art reference teachings to arrive at the claimed invention.
While the above is not an all-inclusive list, the MPEP requires that some rationale be provided in order to support a conclusion of obviousness. In this case, the Office Action failed to present one or more of the above-listed rationales. The rejection lacks a clear articulation of the reasons why these features would allegedly have been obvious and, therefore, the rejection cannot be supported per the requirements set forth by the United States Supreme Court in the KSR decision.
Thus, it is respectfully submitted that the rejection is improper and must be withdrawn.
Further, because the Office Action failed to establish aprima facie case of obviousness, any rejection in a subsequent Office Action providing adequate reasoning(s) would be presented for the first time on the record, and, as such, a subsequent Office Action cannot be made final.
Withdrawal of these rejections are respectfully requested.

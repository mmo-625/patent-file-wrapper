Claims 1-9, 11-16, 18-22 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over Rasmussen in view of Dartey et al, Lengerich et al, the book “ Professional Baking” and Gage et al.
Rasmussen discloses a cookie dough intermediate having and integral design element. The dough intermediate is made from two dough mixes having different identifiable indicia such as different color characteristics or texture characteristics. The inner and outer cookie doughs form and interior cross sectional shape of dough that is visible. The dough intermediate is formed by simultaneously extruding two different doughs through a pair of concentric dies. The co-extruded dough has an internal or center portion dough that is of desired cross sectional shape and encased by a second dough. The dough log is sliced to form individual units. The shape of an animal is shown in figure 3 and 6. ( see col. 1 lines 55-60 and col. 4 lines 47-55)
Rasmussen does not disclose a dough containing shortening with a SFI between about 22-35 at about 70 degree F, dough having similar baked expansion properties the water content of the dough, the ratio of the height and cross-sectional length, integral design that is visible on both sides, the amount of first cookie dough, a third portion of dough, design that is a particular theme such as seasonal, sporting and holiday, sugar having the particle size distribution as claimed, adding inclusion, the type of cutter as claimed, packaging the dough pieces and non-circular external shape.
Dartey et al disclose a cookie dough. They disclose that shortening having a solid fat index of about 15-25% at 70 degree F is commonly used in cookie dough. Dartey et al also disclose that the spread of the cookie dough can be reduced by reducing the proportion of sugar and/or water in the different dough compositions between the inner and outer the dough. Dartey et al also teach the dough or each dough may contain up to about 5% by weight of the leavening system. (see col. 4 lines 60-65, col. 6 lines 47-53, col. 4 lines 26-33)
Lengerich et al disclose cookie dough to form cookies using an extruder; the dough has a water content of from about .5-10%. ( see col. 11 lines 65-66)
Gage et al disclose a process for reducing the time required to achieve texture equilibration in cookies. They teach using sugar having particular particle size for accelerating texture equilibration. Sugar wherein at least about 30% by weight remains on a US Screen mesh of 100 is most preferred for use. ( see col. 7 line 49 through col. 8 line 45)
The book teaches different types of sugar that are used in baked product. It teaches very fine and ultra-fine sugars are prized for making cakes and cookies because they make a more uniform batter and can support higher quantities of fat. It also teaches that finer granulations are better for mixing into doughs and batters because they dissolve more quickly. It also teaches that the particle size of the sugar affect the spread of the cookies. Coarse granulated sugar increases spread, while fine sugar or confectioners’ sugar reduces spread.
It would have been obvious to one skilled in the art to use shortening having the claimed solid fat index because Dartey et al disclose such shortening is normally used in cookie dough. It would have been obvious to combine flour, sugar, water and flour to make the cookie dough because such ingredients are common ingredients for cookie dough as shown by Dartey et al. As to the sugar particles, there are many types of sugar available; for example, there are coarse sugar, fine sugar, very fine sugar etc.. The sugar can also be sized individually by grinding to fit a particular need. The prior art to Gage et al and the cookbook teach that the particle size of the sugar can affect the properties of the cookies. Thus, it would have been obvious to one skilled in the art to size the sugar to obtain the most optimum properties in the cookies made. The appropriate size of sugar is a result-effect variable which can be determined by one skilled in the art to produce the most optimum product. Optimization is within the skill of one in the art. The claimed particle size is conventional as shown by Gage et al. It would have been obvious to add inclusions such as nut, fruit etc.. to enhance the flavor, taste and texture of the cookie product. The water content of cookie dough can vary depending on the type of ingredients used and the texture wanted in the cookies. For example, if syrup or liquid egg or milk, or honey etc.. are used in the dough formula, then it would have been obvious to use less water than in formula without these liquid ingredient. The water content is also affected by the texture desired. A dough producing cookies with soft texture will have more water than dough producing cookies with crispy texture. Since the making of cookies is notoriously well known in the art, it would have been within the skill of one in the art to determine the appropriate amount of water for the type of cookie made. The water content claimed is a conventional amount as shown by Lengerich et al. It would have been obvious to form cookies form doughs with similar expansion if ones wants the cookies to have similar configuration. It would have been readily apparent to one skilled in the art because if one dough portion expands to a high degree in comparison to the other dough portion, the cookies will not be uniform in appearance. This determination would have been readily apparent to one skilled in the art, particularly in view of the teaching of Dartey et al who recognize the importance of reducing the spread of the dough in the different dough portions. Also, the ingredient that is mostly responsible for the spread of the cookies during baking is the leavening agent and Dartey et al teach the two dough portions can have the same amount of leavening agent. Rasmussen does not restrict the dough portions to any specific formulation. Thus, it would have been obvious to one skilled in the art to select any dough portion depending the type of cookie and the appearance wanted. The Rasmussen dough has a particular configuration and is made up of two different doughs. Since the dough intermediate is made by the same method of coextruding the two doughs, it is obvious the dough can be made to have integral design element that is visible on both sides and it would have been obvious to one skilled in the art to do so when desiring to enhance the appearance of the product. It would have been obvious to vary the dough proportion depending on the configuration made, the particular design wanted and the taste, flavor and texture desired. It would also have been obvious to one skilled in the art to change the shape of the dies to make different external configurations and internal designs and to use additional dough portion to form different design elements. These parameters involves routine manipulation of dough equipment and would have been within the determination of one in the art through routine experimentation. There are many variations in making cookies, including different figures, decoration, dough, desire, shape etc.. All these variations are well known in the art and would have been within the skill of one in the art. It would have been obvious to make the dough to fit a particular theme depending on the time of the year and the intended use. For example, if the dough is made during the winter, it would have been obvious to make a Christmas tree or wreath to have a holiday theme. It would have been obvious to use any cutter known in the art to cut the dough and it would have been obvious to package the dough pieces for commercial distribution and storage; this is well known in the art.
In the response filed 5/1/08, applicant argues that the claimed particle size is non-conventional and none of the sugars disclosed in Gage et al include a sugar particle size distribution between about 18-45%. This argument is not persuasive. Applicant states the 100 mesh size in Gate is 59.1%. It is not clear where this calculation comes from. If the percentage is the result of adding the percentage of the different mesh sizes, then the basis of the argument is not understood because Gage et al specifically discloses on column 8 lines 40-45, “ Readily crystallizable sugar having the particle size of Baker’s Special wherein at least about 30% by weight remains on a US Screen mesh of 100 is most preferred”. It is unclear why applicant adds the percentage of different mesh sizes to show that the reference does not teach the percentage with a 100 mesh size. Applicant further argues most of the variety of granulated sugars discussed in Practical Baking is too fine or too coarse. This argument is not persuasive. The position taken in the office action is that the particle sizes of the sugar affects the properties of the cookies. Thus, it would have been obvious to one skilled in the art to select size of sugar to obtain the most optimum properties in the cookies made. The appropriate size of sugar is a result-effect variable which can be determined by one skilled in the art to produce the most optimum product. Optimization is within the skill of one in the art. Applicant has not present any evidence to show why selecting particular sizes of sugar would not have been a matter of optimization where the prior art teaches that different sizes of sugar can be used in cookie formulation and the sizes affect the properties of the sugar. The particle size claimed is conventional as shown by Gage. Applicant’s attention is directed to col. 8 lines 42-45 where Gage et al disclose “ sugar having the particle size of Baker’s Special wherein at least about 30% by weight remains on a US Mesh size of 100 is preferred for use”. Thirty percent is within the claimed range of 18-45%. Thus, in view of both aspects, the claimed sugar size would have been an obvious parameter because the prior art teaches different particle sizes for sugar and the different sizes have an effect on the final outcome of the product. This demonstrates that the size is a result-effective variable and one skilled in the art can readily determine the optimum size through routine experimentation to obtain the properties desired. Applicant states that the Baker’s Special grind is well outside the claimed range in that at least 59.1% grains are capable of being retained on a 100 mesh screen. The basis of this statement is not understood; 59.1% is the total that is retained on 30 mesh, 40 mesh, 50 mesh and 100 mesh. It is not the percent that is retained on 100 mesh. The table A and lines 42-44 clearly state about 30% of the grains are retained on the 100 mesh. Applicant argues that Gage et al and the book do not provide any instruction or suggestion as to how to approach optimization of a dough unit/cookie unit that not only possesses suitable expansion but also must possess suitable extrusion properties. Gage et al and the book are only relied upon to show that sugar have different particle sizes are used in cookie formulation and the sizes give different properties. In any event, the cookie doughs in Gage et al are extruded; thus, this indicate that the size of sugar as claimed can be used in co-extruded dough and Rassmussen teaches co-extruding the dough. Also, the Professional Baking book talks about the sugar size in relation to spreading. This is a teaching of the size affecting the expansion property.
Applicant's arguments filed 5/1/08 have been fully considered but they are not persuasive.

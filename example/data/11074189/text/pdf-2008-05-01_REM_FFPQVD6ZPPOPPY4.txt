Claim Rejections — 35 U.S.C. § 103

Claims 1-9, 11-16, and 18-22 stand rejected under 35 U.S.C. § 103(a) as being
unpatentable over U.S. Patent No. 5,620,713 to Rasmussen in view of U.S. Patent No. 4,894,246
to Dartey et al., U.S. Patent No. 5,079,012 to Lengerich et al., the book “Professional Baking,”
and U.S. Patent No. 4,892,745 to Gage et al. Applicants respectfully traverse the rejections.

As has been discussed in prior responses, none of the above references, alone or in
combination, disclose or suggest the claimed sugar grind, which Applicants discussed in both the
application as filed as well as the Affidavit of December 18, 2006 as being surprising beneficial
to product performance. The claimed sugar grind of independent claims 1, 14, and 20-22
includes a sugar particle size distribution with between about 18% by weight and about 45% by
weight of the sugar being capable of retention with a 100 mesh screen.

The purpose of the limitation “capable of retention with a 100 mesh screen” is to clarify
that the particle size distribution is such that only 18-45% by weight of the total weight of sugar
is retained on a 100 mesh screen, if, for example, the sugar is sifted using only a 100 mesh
screen. One of ordinary skill in the art would recognize that the higher the mesh, the smaller the
hole size. The distribution of sugar that is retained on meshes of lower value (or larger hole
sizes), i.e. 80, 40, 30, is irrelevant with respect to this limitation. If the particle size is large
enough that it is capable of being retained by a 100 mesh screen, it may also be large enough to
be retained by lower mesh screens; however such further distribution limitations are not claimed.

With respect to the teachings of Gage et al., none of the disclosed sugars include a sugar

particle size distribution with between about 18% by weight and about 45% by weight of the

Every one of the “conventional” grinds shown in Table A of Gage et al. satisfies the
definition of being too coarse or too fine for use in the present application. One of skill in the art
reading the disclosure of Gage et al. would not be at all directed to arrive at the presently claimed
dough and instead, would have been directed to utilize sugar grinds that Applicants have
previously described in both the specification and affidavit as being unsuitable for the present
invention.

With reference to the Practical Baking disclosure, most of the variety of granulated
sugars discussed in Practical Baking can be found within Table A of Gage et al., which satisfy
the definition of being too fine or too course for use with the present invention. Practical Baking
is utterly devoid of any discussion relating to custom grinding of sugar to achieve desirable
results for cookie dough that must be extrudable and have suitable expansion properties such that
a continuous design element be recognizable when a dough unit comprising at least two doughs
is baked.

Not only do Gage et al. and Practical Baking fail to disclose the claimed sugar particle
size distribution, nowhere within any of their disclosures are there suggestions that would lead
one of skill in the art to the presently claimed composition. Neither reference addresses the
processing need for a dough that is capable of being extruded so as to form a continuous design
element nor is there any discussion of baked expansion properties that allow the continuous
design element to remain recognizable when baked, let alone when baked from a cookie unit

comprising at least two doughs defining the continuous design element. Instead what is taught

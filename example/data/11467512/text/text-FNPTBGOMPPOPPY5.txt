Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 102
The following is a quotation of the appropriate paragraphs of 35 U.S.C. 102 that form the basis for the rejections under this section made in this Office action:
A person shall be entitled to a patent unless –
(b) the invention was patented or described in a printed publication in this or a foreign country or in public use or on sale in this country, more than one year prior to the date of application for patent in the United States.
1. Claims 1-3, 7, 12, 13, 16, 26 and 29 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 102(b) as being anticipated by U.S. Patent 6,020,892 to Dillon.
With regard to claim 1, Dillon discloses a system, comprising:
at least one motion capture camera configured to capture motion data from a facial surface of an actor using a plurality of sensors disposed on the facial surface of the actor (Fig. 2, cameras 22, 23 and 24, sensors 20); and
a motion capture processor coupled to said at least one motion capture camera (Fig. 2, computer processor 26), said motion capture processor including:
a storage unit (column 4, lines 55-64, Dillon discloses an index for storing all the captured facial expressions);
an image capture module configured to control said at least one motion capture camera to capture frame by frame images of said plurality of sensors disposed on the facial surface of the actor, said image capture module receiving and storing said frame by frame images on said storage unit (column 5, lines 59-67 and column 6, lines 1-14, Dillon discloses real time frame by frame tracking of facial expressions suing the facial markers);
a sensor tracking module configured to retrieve the stored frame by frame images from said storage unit, to perform a tracking function on the images to accurately map said plurality of sensors for each frame, and to provide said motion data from the facial surface (column 6, lines 1-24, Dillon disclose that the tracked facial expressions are used to provide motion data fro animation); and
a digital modeling module configured to receive said motion data to generate a modeled surface representing the facial surface of the actor (column 6, lines 16-29, Dillon discloses using the tracked motion for both animation and robotic face control).
With regard to claim 2, Dillon discloses the system of claim 1, wherein said storage unit, said image capture module, said sensor tracking module, and said digital modeling module of said motion capture processor are configured physically together in a head-mounted system (Fig. 2).
With regard to claim 3, Dillon discloses the system of claim 1, wherein said sensor tracking module and said digital modeling module of said motion capture processor are configured physically separately from said storage unit and said image capture module, which are configured in a head-mounted system (Fig. 2).
With regard to claim 7, Dillon discloses the system of claim 6, further comprising a recorder configured to record motion data captured by said at least one motion capture camera from the facial surface of the actor, wherein said recorder receives record commands from said user workstation (Fig. 2, processor 26 is interpreted to be a workstation used to initialize image capture).
With regard to claim 12, Dillon discloses the system of claim 1, wherein said head-mounted system includes a helmet (Fig. 2, element 21).
With regard to claim 13, Dillon discloses a method of capturing and processing facial motion data, comprising:
coupling a plurality of sensors to target points on a facial surface of an actor (Fig. 2, markers 20);
capturing frame by frame images of said plurality of sensors disposed on the facial surface of the actor using at least one motion capture camera disposed on a head-mounted system (Fig. 2, camera 22 and column 5, lines 59-67 and column 6, lines 1-14, Dillon discloses real time frame by frame tracking of facial expressions suing the facial markers);
performing, in the head-mounted system, a tracking function on said frame by frame images of said plurality of sensors to accurately map said plurality of sensors for each frame (column 6, lines 1-24, Dillon disclose that the tracked facial expressions are used to provide motion data fro animation); and
generating, in the head-mounted system, a modeled surface representing the facial surface of the actor (column 6, lines 16-29, Dillon discloses using the tracked motion for both animation and robotic face control).

Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 103(a) which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
(a) A patent may not be obtained though the invention is not identically disclosed or described as set forth in section 102 of this title, if the differences between the subject matter sought to be patented and the prior art are such that the subject matter as a whole would have been obvious at the time the invention was made to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which said subject matter pertains. Patentability shall not be negatived by the manner in which the invention was made.
2. Claims 4, 6, 8, 14 and 15 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over the combination of U.S. Patents 6,020,892 to Dillon and 6,653,990 to Lestruhaut.
With regard to claim 4, Dillon discloses the system of claim 1, but does not explicitly disclose the features of at least one camera configured to capture images of environment surrounding the actor; and
a display configured to display to the actor the images of environment surrounding the actor captured by said at least one camera, said display capable of also displaying virtual images overlaying on top of the images of environment surrounding the actor to allow the actor to show natural facial expressions substantially synchronized to relevant occurrences.
Lestruhaut discloses these features and teaches a system for displaying real time images overlaid with virtual reality images (column 2, lines 30-60). Therefore it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time of invention to use the virtual reality system of Lestruhaut in combination with the face tracking taught by Dillon in order to record in real time facial expressions as well as real time and virtual reality overlaid images.
With regard to claim 6, Lestruhaut discloses wherein said motion capture processor includes a wireless network interface module configured to wirelessly transmit and receive data to and from a user workstation (column 4, lines 20-35).
With regard to claim 8, Lestruhaut discloses wherein said at least one motion capture camera includes a field splitter configured to deliver multiple fields of view such that stereo vision is achievable without having multiple cameras (column 5, lines 1-10).
With regard to claims 14 and 15, the discussion of claim 4 applies.
3. Claim 9 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over the combination of U.S. Patents 6,020,892 to Dillon and 6,324,296 to McSheery et al.
With regard to claim 9, Dillon discloses the system of claim 1, but does not disclose, wherein each camera of said at least one motion capture camera includes a telecentric lens. McSheery discloses using a telecentric lens camera fro motion capture (column 6, lines 37-49). Therefore it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time of invention to use a telecentric lens camera as taught by McSheery in combination with the motion capture processing of Dillon.
4. Claim 10 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over the combination of U.S. Patents 6,020,892 to Dillon and 6,211,903 to Bullister.
With regard to claim 10, Dillon discloses the system of claim 1, but does not disclose further comprising a mirror configured in one of spherical, hyperbolic, and planar shapes to allow said at least one motion capture camera to capture a reflection of the facial surface, said mirror allowing said at least one motion capture camera to be ergonomically mounted to cause less calibration/stabilization problems on the helmet. Bullister discloses such a mirror configuration (Figs. 2, 3, and 4A). Bullister teaches that such a configuration is more convenient for capturing the user’s face by enabling the user better vision and fro making the image capture device more portable and stable (column 3, lines 24-67). Therefore it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art to use the configuration taught by Bullister to track faces using the method of Dillon to enable better visibility and portability of the system.
5. Claim 11 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over the combination of U.S. Patents 6,020,892 to Dillon and 6,916,096 to Eberl et al.
With regard to claim 11, Dillon discloses the system of claim 1, but does not disclose a plurality of electrodes configured to measure the eye movement and output corresponding signal; and an electrooculogram control unit configured to receive said corresponding signal from said plurality of electrodes and to convert that signal to motion data. Eberl discloses tracking an eye for use in a heads up display for virtual reality and the like (column 10, lines 24-65). Eberl teaches that the technique is useful in tracking eye movement. Therefore it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time of invention to use the eye tracking method taught by Eberl for tracking eye movement in the face tracking method of Dillon.
6. Claims 17-20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over the combination of U.S. Patents 6,020,892 to Dillon and 7,324,110 to Edwards et al.
With regard to claim 17, Dillon discloses the method of claim 13, but does not disclose performing z-depth calculations using grayscale imaging after said capturing frame by frame images of said plurality of sensors. Edwards discloses calculating z-depth values (column 5, lines 10-20). Edwards teaches calculating depth values in order to better determine modeling values. Therefore it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time of invention to calculate depth values as taught Edwards in combination with the tracking method disclosed by Dillon in order to better model the tracked features.
With regard to claim 18, Dillon discloses the method of claim 13, but does not disclose performing 2-D to 3-D triangulation after said capturing frame by frame images of said plurality of sensors. Edwards discloses 2D to 3D triangulation is creating a 3D mesh model (Fig. 1a)
With regard to claim 19, Dillon discloses removing perspective distortion after said capturing frame by frame images of said plurality of sensors (column 5, lines 1-10, Dillon discloses removing bad data or “cleaning.” This is interpreted as removing distortion). Edwards also discloses removing distortion or “cleaning” values (column 2, lines 1-35).
With regard to claim 20, the discussion of claim 19 applies. The cleaning disclosed by Dillon and Edwards is intended to unwarp and better represent the 3D model.
7. Claims 21 and 27 is rejected under 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over the combination of U.S. Patents 6,020,892 to Dillon and 5,926,388 to Kimbrough et al.
With regard to claim 21, Dillon discloses the method of claim 13, but does not disclose illuminating the facial surface of the actor using infrared illumination. Kimbrough discloses using infrared light to illuminate an actor to create relief map (column 6, lines 34-45). Therefore it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time of invention to use IR light as taught by Kimbrough in combination with the tracking and modeling method of Dillon in order to create a model of the actor.
With regard to claim 27, Dillon discloses the method of claim 13, wherein said method comprises coupling a plurality of sensors to target points on a facial surface of an actor, but does not disclose passing an invisible strip of light over the facial surface of the actor using a laser. Edwards discloses using a laser for this purpose (column 6, lines 34-45). Therefore it would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill in the art at the time of invention to use lasers as taught by Edwards in combination with the tracking of sensors taught by Dillon in order to create a facial model.

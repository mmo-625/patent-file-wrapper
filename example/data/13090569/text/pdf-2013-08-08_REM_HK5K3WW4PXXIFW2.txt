REMARKS

This paper is responsive to the final Office Action dated July 2, 2013. No extensions of time

and/or additional claim fees are believed to be due.

consideration. Claims 1, 2, 8, 10 through 13, 15 and 17 have been amended. Support for the

The Drawings
The drawings are objected to under 37 CFR 1.83(a). The drawings must show every

feature of the invention specified in the claims. Therefore, the touch sensor switch (claim
15) must be shown or the feature cancelled from the claims. The term “touch sensor

switch” has been removed from the claim language.

The 35 U.S.C. § 112, second paragraph Rejections:
Claims 1, 2 and 10 through 13 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. § 112(a), second paragraph,

as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject

matter which the inventor regards as the invention.

In claim 1, lines 10-11 the phrase “the digital audio file” does not have proper antecedent
basis. Also, claim 1, line 5 refers to “at least two digital audio files”. Since the at least
two files are not differentiated, it cannot be determined which one is referred to in lines

10-11. Claim 1 has been amended to correct this error.

The 35 U.S.C. § 103(a) Rejections:
Claims | through 5, 8 through 15 and 17 through 20 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. §

103(a) as being unpatentable over U.S. Patent No. 7,356,950 (Avery) in view of U.S.
Patent Application Publication No. 2005/0287913 (Ellman). The Ellman reference is
directed to a toy, such as a doll, which contains an expression mechanism such that user
interaction with said doll can cause the doll’s eyes and mouth to move and to take on
certain facial expressions and the doll may also be capable of responding to a user’s
touch, such as by pressing or rubbing the doll’s chest the doll may simulate laughing or
crying. The present invention is directed to an interactive greeting card which contains
electronics which make the greeting card capable of interacting with a user by emitting
sound in response to the user’s continuous touch or motion applied thereon. Applicant is
of the opinion that the Ellman reference is not analogous art to the present invention
because the doll disclosed by Ellman is a substantive article that cannot be easily placed
into an envelope and mailed to a user. The electronics required to effect sound in

response to user-interaction are completely different in the two cases given that the

greeting card must be relatively lightweight, flat, and inexpensive. The choices one
would make in inventing or designing an electronic solution given these considerations,
would not be the same choices one would make when inventing or designing a child’s toy
or doll. It is obvious from the figures set forth in the Ellman reference, that the internal
components of the doll are quite intricate and substantial compared to the internal
components of the subject greeting card. In addition to the difference in electronics, a
person receiving the doll (as a gift or otherwise) disclosed by Ellman would perhaps

interactive greeting cards wherein the user effects audio by interacting directly with the
greeting card would provide a bigger surprise and entertainment value to the greeting
card recipient. It is far more surprising to receive such an interactive device in such a

small, inexpensive, paper-based product.

Applicant also disagrees with the Examiners opinion that increasing the speed of the
user-applied motion to the greeting card causes an increase in either the speed or volume
of the audio is simply a matter of well-known controls. Of course, controls exist such as
knobs, buttons, dials, etc., however, the same user-interaction which triggers the audio
playback also controls the speed and/or sound. There is no additional controls required to
effectuate these special effects. The user-interactive control also provides the greeting
card recipient with increased entertainment value by increasing the speed of a recorded
voice or animal sound or the volume of a guitar solo. These types of payoffs are not
gained by having a separate device such as a knob or button which controls the volume
and/or speed. None of the cited prior art discloses this feature and the unique
combination of user-interactive sound effects with user-controlled speed and/or volume
in a typical greeting card are novel and non-obvious, especially given that this

combination is not disclosed in the prior art.

In summary, Applicant is of the opinion that adding the user-interactive audio playback

to a typical greeting card is far different, both technically and from an entertainment

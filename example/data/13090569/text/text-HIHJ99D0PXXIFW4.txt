## Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 112
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112(b):
(b) CONCLUSION.—The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor regards as the invention.
The following is a quotation of 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA), second paragraph:
The specification shall conclude with one or more claims particularly pointing out and distinctly claiming the subject matter which the applicant regards as his invention.
Claims 1, 2 and 10-13 are rejected under 35 U.S.C. 112(b) or 35 U.S.C. 112 (pre-AIA), second paragraph, as being indefinite for failing to particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter which the inventor or a joint inventor, or for pre-AIA the applicant regards as the invention.
In claim 1 lines 10-11 “the digital audio file” does not have proper antecedent basis. Line 5 of claim 1 recites “at least two digital audio files." Since the at least two files are not differentiated, it cannot be determined which one is referred to in lines 10-11. Similarly, in claims 10-12 “the digital audio file” is confusing since these claims depend from claim 8 which recites “two or more digital audio files." In claim 13 it is not clear if “a first digital audio file” is one of the “two or more digital audio files” from claim 8 or a further, third, audio file.
In claim 2 “the at least two switches” lacks antecedent basis. Only one switch is recited in claim 1, as amended.
Correction is required.

## Claim Rejections - 35 USC § 103
The following is a quotation of pre-AIA 35 U.S.C. 103(a) which forms the basis for all obviousness rejections set forth in this Office action:
(a) A patent may not be obtained though the invention is not identically disclosed or described as set forth in section 102 of this title, if the differences between the subject matter sought to be patented and the prior art are such that the subject matter as a whole would have been obvious at the time the invention was made to a person having ordinary skill in the art to which said subject matter pertains. Patentability shall not be negatived by the manner in which the invention was made.
Claims 1-5, 8-15 and 17-20 are rejected under pre-AIA 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over Avery in view of Ellman et al. US publication 2005/0287913 A1 (Ellman).
Avery teaches a greeting card comprising body 10 (Figure 1) having a plurality of panels 21, 22A, 22B, with indicia thereon wherein two of the panels (22A and 22B) form enclosed cavity 24 (Figures 2 and 3). Cavity includes an electronics module operative to save and playback at least one audio file upon activation of a slide switch (Figure 6). The card must be open for this switch to be activated.
Avery does not teach a touch sensitive switch which requires constant user interaction to replay the entirety of an audio file however this is well known in the art as shown by Ellman. Ellman teaches a switch [0071] wherein an audio file (laughing) will play in response to rubbing. After the rubbing stops, the audio file (laughing) will stop. It would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art to utilize a touch sensitive switch as shown by Ellman in the display of Avery to provide an improved display and increased entertainment for the recipient (as is taught by Ellman).
Avery and Ellman do not specifically teach the type of messages display by the indicia or played from the memory, however the particular indicia are considered to be entirely a matter of design choice. The examiner takes official notice of many types of greeting cards that include both sung and spoken messages as well as children’s toys and books which provide instructions. It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill to utilize the particular message desired to be conveyed to the recipient.
Regarding claims 11 and 12, Ellman does not specifically teach changing the speed or volume with the touch sensitive switch however these are well known controls for a display. Ellman teaches using the touch switch to provide information to the user (response from the doll). It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill to utilize such a switch for various ways of controlling a display.
Claims 6 and 7 are rejected under pre-AIA 35 U.S.C. 103(a) as being unpatentable over Avery and Ellman as applied to claim 1 above, and further in view of Feuer, US patent 4,200,222 (Feuer).
Avery and Ellman do not teach a card having an aperture in one panel or a die cut piece attached to the body however these features are well known in the art as shown by Feuer. Feuer teaches a card including aperture 24 in one panel for viewing an additional piece 20 attached to the card. It would have been obvious to one of ordinary skill to utilize such additional features in the card of Avery (as modified by Ellman) to provide further entertainment and decoration for the viewer.
